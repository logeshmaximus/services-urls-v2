Controlling the fan and its light: three routes

Separate fan and light control does not always need new wire.

Where a second conductor cannot reach the ceiling box, a receiver in the fan canopy takes signals from a remote or a wall transmitter. The choice is between one wire pulled once and one more part inside the fan.

  • Pull chains at the fan

    The wall switch powers the whole fixture, and the chains set speed and light. Nothing changes in the wall.

  • A two-function wall control

    Fan speed and light, with dimming where the light kit allows it, each get their own control at the switch box.

  • A remote and receiver

    A receiver in the canopy splits fan and light with no new wire, at the cost of batteries and one more part that can fail.

Illustrative photos

Which route fits depends on what reaches the ceiling box today. DC-motor fans usually ship with a remote and offer more speeds than AC-motor fans, which often use pull chains or a wall control.

Site assessment

What gets checked before a fan quote

Most of the price is decided above the ceiling, in the box and what holds it. These are the checks, and why each one matters.

  1. 01

    Is the box marked for a fan?

    A box rated to carry a fan says so on the box, often with a weight limit. Shallow round boxes left from old light fixtures usually carry no such marking.

  2. 02

    What holds the box up

    A fan pulls and twists as it spins. The box has to be fastened to a joist or a brace, not just to plaster, lath or drywall.

  3. 03

    The ceiling material

    Plaster and lath, drywall, wood plank and concrete slab each take a different mount. Plaster can crack when an opening is enlarged.

  4. 04

    Height and slope

    Fan makers' instructions usually call for blades at least 7 feet above the floor. Ceiling height sets the downrod, and a slope needs an angled mount.

  5. 05

    Room around the blades

    Walls, cabinet doors, bunk beds and nearby light fixtures all need clearance from the blade tips. A doorway that swings into the fan's path is a common catch.

  6. 06

    The wires at the box

    Years of heat from incandescent bulbs can leave wire insulation brittle where it enters the box. A ground path for the fan's metal parts is checked too.

  7. 07

    How many wires reach the switch

    Checked at the switch box, since it decides which control route fits.

If the box cannot carry a fan

  1. 1

    A fan brace through the existing opening

    An expanding bar slides in between two joists through the hole the old box used, so the ceiling usually stays closed.

  3. 2

    A fan-rated box fixed to a joist

    Where a joist sits right at the spot, a rated box fastens straight to it, sometimes after moving the fan slightly.

  5. 3

    A concrete slab ceiling

    In a high-rise, the box set into the concrete is checked for a fan rating. Where it has none, support listed for fans or a different spot is the answer.

Which route fits depends on the joist direction, the ceiling material and where the fan should hang.

Repair, rebalance or replace?

A wobble or a rattle is usually a repair, not a new fan.

  • Balancing the blades and tightening the blade screws and bracket settle most shakes.

  • A hum, a single working speed or a dead remote often comes down to one part, such as a capacitor, a receiver or the wall control.

  • Replacement makes sense when the motor has failed, parts are no longer made, or the household wants a different size or a light.

A fan a previous owner put up

Many Chicago homes change hands with fans already on the ceilings, and nothing on the fan shows what holds it up. A fan that sways as a whole, or a canopy that shifts when touched, points to the box rather than the blades. The fan's make and model, from the label on top of the motor, helps when parts are needed.

01Does a new ceiling fan always need a new ceiling box?

No. A box marked for fan support and fastened to a joist or brace can carry a new fan with no ceiling work. A brace or new box comes in only when the old one was made for a light or has worked loose. On a ceiling with a sound, rated box, the job is the fan itself, with no ceiling work to price.

02What size ceiling fan suits a room?

Blade span follows floor area. A bedroom or office up to about 144 square feet usually suits a fan of 36 to 42 inches across. A room up to about 225 square feet suits around 44 inches, and a large living room 50 to 54 inches. Past about 400 square feet, two fans often work better than one.

03Which way should the blades turn in summer and in winter?

In summer, most fans should turn counterclockwise seen from below, pushing air down for a cooling breeze. In winter, a slow clockwise turn draws air up and moves warm air off a high ceiling without a draft. The switch is usually a small slider on the motor housing, and some remotes have it too. The fan should stop fully before the direction changes.

04Can a ceiling fan go in a bathroom or on a porch?

Yes, with the right rating. A covered porch calls for a fan listed for damp locations, and a spot rain can reach calls for one listed for wet locations. In a bathroom, NEC 410.10(D) keeps every part of a paddle fan out of a zone around the tub or shower. That zone runs 3 feet sideways and 8 feet up from the rim or threshold. Clearing steam is a job for an exhaust fan instead.

05What should the household check before buying a fan?

The fan's weight, size and mounting parts matter as much as its look. A heavy fan needs a box rated for its weight. A downrod or sloped-ceiling kit missing from the carton can mean a second trip. Whether Highlights Chicago supplies the fan or hangs one the household bought is worth settling when the job is booked.

06Who may install a ceiling fan in Chicago?

A City-licensed electrical contractor. The City's electrical contractor license page was read on October 19, 2026. It says a license is needed to install, alter or maintain wiring or equipment the Chicago Electrical Code covers. That applies whether or not a permit is required. The page's only exceptions cover public utility equipment and State-licensed alarm contractors. Highlights Chicago's two City licenses are listed among the reasons on this page.

07Does a ceiling fan need its own circuit?

Usually not. A household ceiling fan draws little current, so it normally shares the lighting circuit that already feeds the ceiling box. A fan in a new spot is often fed from a nearby box on an existing circuit, where the load allows. A dedicated circuit comes up mainly for very large fans or several fans on one busy circuit.

08Is a wobbling or noisy fan an emergency?

Usually not. A gentle wobble or a click is most often balance or a loose screw. A fan that drops away from the ceiling, sparks or smells of burning is different: switch it off at the breaker and leave it off. Highlights Chicago offers emergency electrical service 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

How ceiling fans fit Chicago ceilings and wiring

A ceiling fan hangs where a light once did, but it weighs more, moves, and often needs two controls. In Chicago, the ceiling it hangs from usually predates the rules written for fans.

Why are so many older Chicago ceiling boxes not rated for a fan?

Most of Chicago's houses and small flats were built for ceiling lights, not fans. Of Chicago's single-family and 2 to 6 unit buildings, 58.6% were built before 1940. Among the 2 to 6 unit buildings alone, the share is 88.5% (Cook County Assessor, tax year 2025).

A light fixture hangs still. A fan is heavier, and it pulls and twists its box every time it runs. The NEC answered that in section 314.27(C), which requires a box that is a fan's only support to be listed for that use. The Chicago Electrical Code is based on the NEC.

Boxes put in decades earlier were never tested for that load. Many are shallow pans nailed to lath or hung from a single strap. They can hold a light for a century yet work loose under a fan.

Highlights Chicago has met this in older buildings. Ria H., Dec 2021, describes the fans in an old Chicago two-flat working loose from the ceiling, and its crew servicing and anchoring them all.

Why does Chicago's wiring matter on a fan job?

Chicago has long required most wiring to run in raceway such as metal conduit. Armored cable is allowed only for limited runs, such as those fished into existing walls. The plastic-sheathed cable common in many suburbs is allowed only in narrow cases.

That history helps a fan job. Conduit is a pipe, so new wire for a fan control can often be pulled through it without opening plaster.

The metal pipe also often serves as the ground path back to the panel. In older buildings with early armored cable or knob-and-tube wiring, that path can be missing or unreliable, which matters for a fan's metal housing.

Reviews describe Highlights Chicago working with older wiring. Linda M., Jun 2025, describes a condo with old ceiling connections that its crew adapted to a new fan. Patience N., Jun 2025, describes fans hardwired to the wall switch instead of relying on the remote alone.

What changes when the fan goes into a condo building?

Mostly the paperwork and the timing. Associations in Chicago high-rises and converted buildings often set rules on working hours, freight elevator bookings and contractor sign-in.

Many also ask for a certificate of insurance before work starts, sometimes naming the association. Building management can take a few days to process it, so it is worth asking early.

Condo declarations often treat the structure above a unit's ceiling as common property. That is why some associations want to hear about a new brace or box, even on a small job.

Reviews describe Highlights Chicago on condo fan jobs. Lulu Z., Aug 2023, lists new ceiling fans among the tasks in a downtown high-rise condo. Bob B., Oct 2025, describes it providing insurance and license details for a job in a large River North building.

How does a fan go up on an open loft ceiling?

Many Chicago lofts sit in converted factory and warehouse buildings. Their ceilings are often exposed timber joists or concrete, far higher than a typical flat's.

An open ceiling has no hidden cavity to fish wire through. The feed to a new fan usually runs in conduit along a joist or across the surface, and the box fastens straight to the structure.

That conduit stays in view on the finished ceiling, so its route is worth agreeing before work starts.
