Panel swap or service upgrade?

A new panel and a new service are two different jobs.

A panel replacement changes the box of breakers and keeps what feeds it. A service upgrade also replaces the meter socket, the service conductors and, on an overhead service, the mast and weatherhead.

  • Panel swap, same rating

    For a worn, damaged or full panel on a service that can still carry the home.

  • Service upgrade

    When the calculated load is more than the service can carry.

  • Service plus utility work

    A much larger service, where ComEd may also have to change its drop or the transformer behind it.

Site assessment

What the walkthrough checks before a panel is sized

Most of the scope and the price is settled by what is already on the wall and outside.

  1. 01

    The calculated load

    A load calculation under NEC Article 220 adds up what the home runs and plans to run. Chicago's Title 14E incorporates the 2017 edition of that code (chicago.gov, read October 17, 2026).

  2. 02

    Every rating in the service

    The main breaker, the meter socket and the service conductors each carry a rating. The lowest of the three sets the real size of the service.

  3. 03

    The meter socket

    Its rating, its condition and whether ComEd can reach it decide whether it stays or changes.

  4. 04

    Overhead or underground entrance

    An overhead drop lands on a mast and weatherhead; an underground line comes up through a wall or floor. Each one changes the outside work.

  5. 05

    Grounding and bonding

    Older Chicago buildings often relied on the metal water pipe alone. The code calls for a supplemental electrode as well, and the bonds to water and gas piping get checked.

  6. 06

    Heat at the lugs and breakers

    Discolored metal, melted insulation or a hot spot on an infrared scan points to a loose or overloaded connection.

  7. 07

    Panel condition

    Rust, scorch marks or a damaged bus are physical reasons to replace a panel.

When the panel is full or the service is short

  1. 1

    Rearrange or add a subpanel

    When the service has spare amps but the panel has no spaces, a subpanel or breakers listed for that panel add room.

  3. 2

    Manage the new load

    A charger set to a lower current, or a load management device, can keep a new load inside the existing service.

  5. 3

    Upgrade the service

    See panel swap or service upgrade above.

Repair, replace or upgrade?

The fault decides the fix.

  • A loose lug, one failed breaker or a corroded neutral is a repair.

  • Heat damage, water damage or breakers no longer made mean a new panel.

Leaving room for solar, a battery or a generator

A panel sized only for today can run out of room for a transfer switch or a solar breaker, and its bus rating caps how much solar it accepts. Naming those plans first lets the new panel fit those plans, rather than changing it twice.

01Does a panel problem always mean a full service upgrade?

No. Several cheaper routes keep the existing service. A loose lug or a failed breaker can be repaired. A subpanel can add spaces. A new charger can be set to a lower current. Highlights Chicago also offers breaker and panel repair, so the smaller fix stays on the table when it fits. The site assessment above shows what decides it.

02Can the panel move to a new spot during the upgrade?

Often, within code limits. NEC 110.26 calls for clear space in front of a panel, generally 30 inches wide and 3 feet deep. NEC 240.24 keeps panels out of bathrooms and clothes closets. The cost side is in the price drivers above; a move pairs best with a service upgrade.

03Does a new panel mean rewiring the whole house?

Usually not. A new panel reconnects the circuits already in the walls, each on a breaker sized for its wire. Rewiring is a separate job, needed where old cloth or knob-and-tube wiring has failed or cannot be protected. Highlights Chicago also handles wiring and rewiring, and that work is best priced on its own line, so the panel price stays clear.

04Can a panel upgrade be done in a Chicago winter?

Yes. Panel work runs all year, but a winter cutover takes more planning. The furnace blower, the boiler controls, the sump pump and the freezer all stop while the power is off. A daylight cutover, a charged battery backup for the sump pump and a plan for medical equipment keep the outage manageable.

05What happens after a home inspector or insurer flags the panel?

A home inspection report notes what looks old or unusual and usually asks for a licensed electrician to evaluate it. That evaluation decides between repair and replacement. In a July 2018 review, Audrey Y. describes Highlights Chicago fixing a panel issue from a home inspection the same day. An email with a detailed account of the work followed, so the buyers could be shown the problem was fixed.

06Can the homeowner pull the meter or open the panel to save time?

No. The wires feeding a meter stay live even with the main breaker off. Pulling one is a job for ComEd, or for a licensed electrician working with ComEd. Inside the panel, the parts ahead of the main breaker stay live too. Resetting a tripped breaker once is fine; a breaker that trips again needs an electrician.

07Will a bigger service raise the electric bill?

Not on its own. The amp rating is capacity, not consumption, and the bill follows the energy the home actually uses. What raises a bill is the new load behind the upgrade, such as a car charger or central air. A larger service simply lets those loads run without tripping the main breaker.

08Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Guide 04 / 04

How a panel upgrade fits a Chicago building

Four questions the blocks above leave open, each with the Chicago detail behind it.

Can a year of real usage show that a Chicago home has enough amps?

Sometimes. The code lets an existing home be judged on its measured peak demand instead of a calculated estimate. That can show room for a charger without a new service.

A 100-amp service is still common in Chicago houses that heat and cook with gas. Those homes often draw far less than the breaker ratings suggest, because big loads rarely run together.

The usual response to a new charger or heat pump is a standard load calculation, which can come out short on paper. The household then hears that only 200 amps will do.

The code offers a second path for an existing building. NEC 220.87 lets the existing load come from a full year of maximum demand data, multiplied by 125 percent. If that figure plus the new load fits, the service can stay.

Either result points somewhere useful. Where a year of data shows room, the charger can stay on the existing service. Where it does not, the upgrade is the honest answer. ComEd's smart-meter usage history is one source for that year of data.

Camille M.'s December 2025 review describes a Tesla charger in a detached garage installed together with a Highlights Chicago panel upgrade. The work passed inspection.

Why does the outside half of a service upgrade set the date?

Because ComEd reconnects a changed service only when the building's side meets its requirements. The meter is ComEd's, but the meter socket, the mast and the weatherhead belong to the building.

On an alley-fed Chicago lot, ComEd's wires cross from a pole to a weatherhead. From there the building's own wires run down a steel mast to the meter socket. On an older building, each part may date from an earlier service.

Quotes often price the basement panel and leave the outside vague. On the day, a rusted socket or a mast pulled loose by ice becomes a surprise charge or a second visit.

ComEd's requirements set the order of work. So the outside parts belong in the scope from the first visit.

A Highlights Chicago quote can name the socket, the mast and the weatherhead beside the panel. The City's Express Permit Program files a service and its panel under one work subtype, "New service or service disconnecting means (panel)".

Kelly L.'s November 2025 review is that case. A front house and a coach house both needed new risers and weatherheads before ComEd would upgrade the service. Highlights Chicago dealt with ComEd for the owner.

What changes when the panel is in a two-flat or a condo building?

The service is shared. Each unit usually has its own panel and its own meter in a shared meter stack. A house panel feeds the halls, and one service feeds the whole building. Associations usually ask for proof of insurance first; Highlights Chicago's Yelp business page lists it as licensed and insured.

Chicago's flat buildings are old. The Cook County Assessor dates 88.5% of the city's two to six unit buildings to before 1940.

The usual pattern is piecemeal. One unit gets a new panel when it adds air conditioning. The shared service, the meter bank and the house panel stay unchanged.

The City's paperwork looks at the building. Its Express Permit page asks for a condo association approval letter for work on condo property, even inside one unit.

With insurance papers and the letter in hand, the meters, the house panel and the unit feeders can be planned together, so the association approves the building once.

Michael C.'s December 2020 review shows the unit-level pattern. A second opinion on a subpanel for floor heating changed the plan. Boz, the owner of Highlights Chicago, recommended moving the unit's panel out of the shared basement and into the unit.

Does a fuse box or an older panel with a poor safety record have to go?

Not every one, and not on age alone. A fuse box sized for today's loads, with the right fuses, still protects its circuits. Some older panel lines from makers no longer in business have breakers with a documented record of failing to trip. Replacement is the usual advice for those.

Panel upgrades on Chicago houses and small flat buildings happen mostly in old buildings. Of upgrade permits matched to an Assessor residential record, 59.6% are on buildings from before 1940. That is close to the 58.6% share among the city's houses and two to six unit buildings.

Two bad answers are common. One replaces every old box on sight. The other keeps a fuse service running long after the home's loads have outgrown it.

The real tests are capacity, condition and parts. A 60-amp fuse service rarely has room for central air or a charger. Breakers for discontinued panels are hard to find, and each must be listed for that panel.
