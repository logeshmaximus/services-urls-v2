GFCI, AFCI or dual-function: what changes?

All three have a test button, but they watch for different faults. NEC 210.8 covers GFCI protection and NEC 210.12 covers AFCI protection; Chicago's amendments in Title 14E decide where each one applies.

Site assessment

What gets checked before a GFCI goes in

The device itself is the quick part. These checks decide whether it will protect anything, and whether it will stay reset.

  1. 01

    Finding the first box on the run

    The outlets are traced back to the first box on the circuit before a device is chosen.

  2. 02

    Supply and load wires

    The incoming and outgoing wires must land on the right terminals. Reversed, a newer GFCI will not reset; an older one can power the outlet without protecting it.

  3. 03

    A ground, or none

    A tester and a look in the box show whether there is a ground wire, grounded metal conduit, or no ground at all.

  4. 04

    Box depth and condition

    A GFCI is deeper than a plain outlet. A shallow or crowded old box may need replacing before the device fits.

  5. 05

    Damp and wet spots

    Outdoors, on decks and on rooftops, the device has to be weather-resistant and sit under a cover rated for wet locations.

  6. 06

    Shared neutrals

    Older multi-unit wiring can share one neutral between two circuits, which makes a GFCI trip on loads it does not feed.

  7. 07

    Where the reset will be

    NEC 210.8 in the 2017 edition asks for the GFCI in a readily accessible spot, not behind a refrigerator or a washer.

If a GFCI outlet will not fit the spot

  1. 1

    A GFCI breaker

    Protects the whole circuit from the panel when the box is too shallow or the first outlet is hard to reach.

  3. 2

    Protection from the first box

    A GFCI in the first outlet box, where that box is easier to reach than the panel.

  5. 3

    A deeper box

    Replacing the old box, so the device has room.

Which route fits depends on the panel make, the free breaker spaces and the order the outlets are wired in.

Fix the circuit, or replace the device?

A GFCI that trips is usually doing its job, so a new device alone just moves the problem.

  • The circuit and what is plugged into it are checked first.

  • A device that will not test, will not reset with power present, or runs warm is replaced.

  • Most GFCIs made since mid-2015 run a self-test, and many signal when they reach end of life.

Dead outlets, and no idea which GFCI feeds them

In older Chicago homes one GFCI, in a bathroom, the basement or the garage, often protects outlets in other rooms with no label to say so. When outlets die and no breaker has tripped, that hidden device is the usual reason. Tracing the run finds it. Labeling the protected outlets, or moving the reset somewhere easier to reach, can follow.

01Does every outlet in an older Chicago home have to become a GFCI?

No. GFCI protection is required in named locations, listed in the next answer. Outlets elsewhere can stay as they are. When an outlet in a listed location is replaced, NEC 406.4(D)(3) asks for GFCI protection on the new one. Highlights Chicago works to the Chicago Electrical Code, which has the final word on each location in a Chicago home.

02Where does a Chicago home need GFCI protection?

Chicago's code builds on the 2017 NEC. Its section 210.8(A) covers 15- and 20-amp, 125-volt outlets in bathrooms, garages, outdoors, crawl spaces and unfinished basements. Laundry areas and kitchen counters are on the list, as are outlets within 6 feet of a sink, tub or shower. So is a dishwasher outlet. Chicago's amendments can change details, so the City's code text decides.

03Can a refrigerator or chest freezer plug into a GFCI outlet?

Under the 2017 NEC, a kitchen refrigerator outlet that does not serve the counter and sits more than 6 feet from the sink is outside 210.8(A)(6) and (7). A garage, laundry or unfinished basement outlet is on the list, whatever plugs in. A healthy appliance rarely trips a modern GFCI, and a plug-in temperature alarm guards the food if one does.

04Does a Chicago rooftop deck need its own outlet?

The 2017 NEC, in section 210.52(E)(3), calls for at least one outlet on an attached deck, balcony or porch reached from inside the home. Outdoors, that outlet needs GFCI protection. On a Chicago roof the harder question is the route. Conduit may run up a stairwell, past a roof hatch or along a brick parapet, and that route usually decides what Highlights Chicago quotes.

05Can a homeowner replace a GFCI outlet in Chicago?

No. The City's Electrical Contractor License page says a City license is required to install, alter or maintain any wiring or equipment the Chicago Electrical Code regulates. That applies whether or not a permit is required (chicago.gov, read October 17, 2026). Utilities and State-licensed alarm contractors are the exceptions. Highlights Chicago is a City-licensed electrical contractor.

06Who handles GFCI work in a Chicago condo building?

It depends on where the outlet is. Outlets inside a unit are usually the owner's, while hallway, laundry room, roof deck and garage outlets are often common elements the association controls. The declaration and bylaws set the line. The board's rules can also limit when work happens in shared spaces, so it helps to settle whose outlet it is before booking.

07Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. Guide 04 / 04

How GFCI protection fits a Chicago building

A GFCI compares the current leaving on one wire with the current returning on the other. When about 5 milliamps goes missing, it opens the circuit almost at once. These questions decide how that plays out in older Chicago wiring.

Can a GFCI stand in for the missing ground in a pre-1940 Chicago building?

Often, yes. Under the 2017 NEC, which Chicago's code builds on, a GFCI can protect a two-wire circuit with no ground. The outlets just need the right labels. It guards people against shock, but it gives surge strips and electronics no ground. For Highlights Chicago, a City-licensed contractor, the choice turns on what will be plugged in.

More than half of Chicago's houses and small residential buildings, 58.6% by Cook County Assessor records, were built before 1940. Many were wired with two conductors and no ground wire, so their outlets take two-prong plugs. Some later runs went in metal conduit, which can carry a ground of its own.

The common shortcut is a three-prong outlet fixed to the old two wires. The plug fits and the outlet looks modern, but nothing behind it is grounded. NEC 406.4(D)(2) does not allow that swap.

The code gives three legal routes instead. One is another two-prong outlet. Another is a GFCI outlet marked No Equipment Ground. The third is three-prong outlets fed from a GFCI, each marked GFCI Protected and No Equipment Ground. Where metal conduit tests as a continuous path back to the panel, a grounded outlet may be possible without new wire.

Highlights Chicago's reviews describe detailed estimates and written proposals before work starts. On a two-wire circuit, the choice is made outlet by outlet. Do the national GFCI rules apply in Chicago?

Not all of them. Chicago's Electrical Code builds on the 2017 NEC, while most online advice follows the 2020 or 2023 editions, which widened GFCI rules. Highlights Chicago works to the Chicago code, the one a City electrical inspector applies. The City's code text, not a national checklist, decides what a Chicago outlet needs.

Chicago writes its own electrical code. The City's construction codes page (read October 17, 2026) says Title 14E builds on provisions of the 2017 National Electrical Code. Local amendments sit on top.

National articles usually describe the newest edition. The 2020 NEC moved GFCI protection to every kitchen outlet, not only those serving the counter, and to many 240-volt outlets up to 50 amps. The 2023 edition went further.

So a Chicago homeowner may hear that a dryer or range outlet must be GFCI when the 2017 rules do not ask for it. The reverse happens too, and an outlet the Chicago code covers gets missed. Mixing editions also muddles an inspection list at a home sale.

Why does a sump pump trip a GFCI, and what should a Chicago basement do about it?

A sump pump on a GFCI can trip during a storm, just when the basement needs it. The 2017 NEC still puts unfinished basement outlets on the GFCI list. The better answer is a pump on its own protected circuit with a high-water alarm, and often a battery backup pump. A Highlights Chicago review below shows how such a job can grow.

Heavy summer storms push water into Chicago basements, and a sump pump is the first line of defense in many of them. The pump usually plugs into an outlet near the pit, in the dampest corner of the house.

After a nuisance trip, the usual fix is a plain outlet in place of the GFCI. Another is an extension cord from an outlet elsewhere. Both remove shock protection in the wettest room of the house.

An old pump motor that leaks a few milliamps will trip a healthy GFCI. That usually points to a pump wearing out, not a GFCI that is wrong. On its own GFCI-protected circuit, a pump stops tripping from a freezer or dehumidifier on the same line. A battery high-water alarm warns if it does stop.
