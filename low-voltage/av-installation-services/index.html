Where the equipment lives: three layouts

Most of an AV wiring job is decided by where the electronics sit.

Every speaker and screen is wired back to that spot, so moving it changes every cable run and the circuit that feeds it.

  • In the room

    A console under the TV holds the electronics. Short runs, and one outlet does most of the work.

  • A closet or utility room

    A central rack serves several rooms. Every speaker run goes back there, and the rack usually wants its own circuit.

  • The basement

    In a two-flat or bungalow, the basement is often the easiest place to reach the panel and to drop cable up into the rooms above.

Site assessment

What gets checked before an AV wiring quote

Most of the price is set by the walls, the ceilings and the panel, not by the speakers. This is what gets looked at, and why.

  1. 01

    The wall type

    Plaster and lath, drywall and brick each take cable differently. Plaster cracks if it is cut carelessly, and brick has little space behind it.

  2. 02

    Blocking inside the walls

    Fire blocking and floor plates stop a cable dropped from the attic. Each one found adds an opening or a different route.

  3. 03

    Access above and below

    An unfinished basement or a reachable attic makes fishing cable much easier. A finished basement ceiling removes the easiest route.

  4. 04

    Room in the panel

    A dedicated circuit needs a free breaker space. A full panel can need a tandem breaker, a subpanel or an upgrade.

  5. 05

    What shares the circuit now

    A TV wall often sits on a bedroom or living room circuit that also feeds lights and a space heater. That matters once an amplifier joins it.

  6. 06

    Grounding of the cable TV line

    Coax should be tied into the building's grounding system where it enters. A missing ground connection is a common source of hum.

  7. 07

    Power and signal kept apart

    NEC 725.136 keeps low-voltage cable out of the same box or raceway as power wiring, unless a barrier divides them.

  8. 08

    Building rules

    Condo associations often set rules on opening walls, working hours and contractor insurance. Those rules can decide the route.

If a wall cannot be opened

  1. 1

    Through a closet or chase

    A closet stacked above another, or an old chimney chase, can carry cable between floors without touching the main rooms.

  3. 2

    Along the baseboard or crown

    A painted raceway at the floor or ceiling line carries the cable around the room where the wall itself is brick.

  5. 3

    Wait for the renovation

    When a kitchen or bathroom remodel is planned, running the cable while those walls are open costs less than fishing it now.

Which route fits depends on the walls, the access and the building rules.

Repair, rerun or add a circuit?

A loose wall plate connection or a worn outlet is a repair.

  • A cable damaged inside a finished wall is usually left in place and replaced with a new run.

  • Equipment on a busy circuit needs a circuit of its own, never a bigger breaker on the same wire.

  • Never cure hum by removing the ground pin or using a ground-lift adapter, because the ground is a safety path.

Wiring left by an earlier owner or installer

Many Chicago homes have speaker wire and wall plates from an older system, with no map of where anything goes. Each run can be traced and tested, then reused, relabeled or abandoned. Photos of the wall plates and of the old equipment location help.

01Does whole home audio installation mean opening up every wall?

No. Most finished Chicago houses and two-flats can be wired through basements, attics and stacked closets. Openings are usually limited to the speaker and wall plate locations, plus a few spots where blocking stops a cable. A solid brick wall may take surface raceway instead, which needs no patching at all.

02Does a wall-mounted TV need its own circuit?

Usually not. One display and a streaming box draw little power, and an existing outlet circuit often has room. A rack with an amplifier, a projector or a powered subwoofer is different, because its load adds to whatever already shares the circuit. Highlights Chicago adds a dedicated circuit where the load calls for one.

03Can a TV's power cord be hidden inside the wall?

Not the cord itself. NEC 400.12, adopted in the Chicago Electrical Code, does not allow flexible cords to run through walls or ceilings. The permanent answer is a recessed outlet behind the display, wired as part of a branch circuit, with a separate low-voltage pass-through for video and network cable. Highlights Chicago installs that outlet as permanent wiring.

04Does AV wiring need a City permit in Chicago?

New circuits and outlets are line-voltage work, and in Chicago that goes on a City electrical permit. Speaker and video cable is low-voltage work, and whether a home job needs a permit for it depends on the scope. Unpermitted line-voltage work tends to surface later, during a home sale or an insurance claim.

05Can in-ceiling speakers go into a high-rise condo?

Often only where there is a dropped ceiling or a soffit. Many Chicago high-rises have concrete ceilings with no cavity above them, so speakers go into soffits, into walls or onto the surface. The association usually has rules on hours, noise and insurance, and its approval comes before any wall is opened.

06Are wireless speakers a way to avoid wiring?

Partly. Wireless speakers skip the speaker cable but still need power at every location, which can mean new outlets in ceilings or high on walls. Wired passive speakers need only cable, powered from one amplifier at the equipment location. Highlights Chicago wires the outlets and circuits for either approach.

07Does a ceiling projector need an outlet in the ceiling?

Usually, yes. A ceiling-mounted projector needs power at the mount, and the rule against cords inside ceilings applies there too. So the outlet goes in the ceiling beside the mount, and the video cable runs separately to the equipment location. In a concrete condo ceiling, surface raceway is often the only route.

08Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Guide 04 / 04

How AV wiring fits an older Chicago building

An AV system in an older Chicago building is mostly a question of routes and power. The sections below cover the building, the permit data, the trades involved and planning ahead.

Why do Chicago plaster and brick walls change an AV job?

Most of Chicago's houses and small apartment buildings are old. Cook County Assessor records for tax year 2025 show that 58.6% of the city's single-family and 2 to 6 unit buildings predate 1940. Among the 2 to 6 unit buildings alone, the share is 88.5%.

Those buildings have plaster on wood lath, solid brick outer walls with a thin furred space behind the plaster, and fire blocking between floors. Cable that slides through a drywall stud bay in minutes can stop dead in a plaster wall, at a lath strip or a fire block.

Highlights Chicago has City permits on record for every year since 2012. Its permits (all years) are concentrated in West Town (175), Logan Square (120), Near West Side (83) and Lake View (78). In West Chicago township, which includes West Town, 83.0% of houses and small buildings predate 1940.

Does Chicago permit data show what home AV costs?

No. City of Chicago permit data has no category for home audio and video, so no reported median describes a TV wall or a speaker system.

The nearest grouping is broad. In the 24 months to September 2026, 2,093 Express Permit Program electrical-scope permits had descriptions matching low voltage, CCTV and cabling work. Most used the City form's low voltage and communication option. They cover cameras, data networks, access control and intercoms in every kind of building.

Low-voltage and cabling permits are also a small part of Highlights Chicago's own record. On an AV job, its permitted work is usually the circuits and outlets the equipment plugs into.

Who does which part of a home AV project?

A finished system usually involves three or four parties. The internet or cable provider brings service to the building. Someone has to choose, mount and program the equipment. An electrical contractor handles the circuits, the outlets and the permitted wiring.

Trouble starts at the handoffs. Nobody labels the cables, nobody fixes where the rack will sit, and the equipment arrives before the wall plates match it.

Highlights Chicago works on the electrical side of that split as a licensed and insured electrical contractor. Its Google reviews name the people who do the work, including Boz, Raul, Jimmy and Victor.

What should be wired now for equipment that comes later?

Speakers and screens get replaced every few years, and the next ones may need a different cable. Walls in an old Chicago building are costly to open twice.

The simplest safeguard is empty conduit. Flexible conduit from the TV wall down to the basement, or from a closet up to the attic, lets new cable be pulled later. The wall stays closed. A pull string left inside makes that easy.

Spare boxes at likely screen heights cost little while the walls are open. So do a second cable to each speaker zone and an outlet where a future rack could sit. They cost far more once the plaster is back.
