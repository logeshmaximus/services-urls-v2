Linking CO alarms: three routes

The City's CO page calls alarms that link with smoke alarms allowed, not required.

Linking helps where one alarm sounding far away may not wake anyone, such as a basement boiler room.

  • A wired link

    A third conductor runs between hardwired alarms, so one alarm going off sets off the rest.

  • A radio link

    Alarms talk to each other wirelessly. Each still needs its own power where it is mounted.

  • Stand-alone alarms

    Each alarm sounds on its own. This suits a small unit where every alarm can be heard from the bedrooms.

Site assessment

What decides where CO alarms go in a Chicago home

The City's carbon monoxide page, read October 17, 2026, sets these rules. They decide whether a home needs alarms, how many, and where.

  1. 01

    Fuel-burning equipment in the home

    A gas or oil furnace, boiler, water heater, dryer, range or cooktop, or a wood or gas fireplace, puts a home under the rule. A unit with all-electric heat and appliances and no attached garage needs none.

  2. 02

    Ducts shared with another space

    A home joined by air ducts to another part of the building that holds a fuel-burning appliance or fireplace counts too, even with no gas in the unit.

  3. 03

    A garage beside or below

    A door straight into a garage, or a home built over one, brings the rule in unless the garage is ventilated on its own.

  4. 04

    Each bedroom's 15-foot zone

    The Illinois Act and the City both put an alarm within 15 feet of every room used for sleeping. Bedrooms at opposite ends of a unit can each need one.

  5. 05

    A fireplace or fuel appliance in a bedroom

    That bedroom also takes an alarm inside it.

  6. 06

    Furnace, boiler and water heater rooms

    An alarm goes in the same room as each fuel-burning furnace, boiler and water heater, which in Chicago is often a basement.

  7. 07

    Height and spots to avoid

    The City advises a wall spot at least 5 feet up, or the ceiling. An alarm should not sit right next to or over a fireplace, stove or other open flame.

When a required spot has no power nearby

  1. 1

    A short run from a nearby box

    Power is taken from a live box on the same circuit, through the ceiling or wall, where the circuit has room.

  3. 2

    Surface conduit

    Metal conduit along the wall or ceiling, where opening old plaster is not wanted.

  5. 3

    A battery alarm at that one spot

    In a home where the City allows battery power, a sealed-battery unit can cover a spot that wiring cannot reach easily.

Which route fits depends on the plaster, the circuit and the age of the home.

When it is the alarm, and when it is the wiring

A chirp usually points to a battery or an alarm past its date, and neither needs new wiring.

  • Wiring work comes in when a hardwired alarm has lost power, when linked alarms stop sounding together, or when a required spot has no power.

  • Highlights Chicago offers emergency electrical service 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, for a hardwired alarm that goes dark at night.

A plug-in alarm at baseboard height

Many plug-in CO alarms end up in a receptacle a foot off the floor, well below the height the City advises in the checklist above. Carbon monoxide often mixes with warm, rising air, which is why the City points alarms higher. A receptacle moved up the wall, or a hardwired or sealed-battery alarm at that height, follows the City's advice.

01Does a pre-2018 Chicago home have to switch to hardwired CO alarms?

No. The City's CO page says alarms in houses and apartments built before 2018 may run on a removable battery. Homes built since 2018 need alarms wired to building power, with a removable back-up battery. Highlights Chicago can still swap battery units for hardwired ones in an older home. Only the back-up battery is then left to look after, but the City does not require the change.

02Is one combination smoke and CO alarm enough?

Sometimes. The City allows combination units but does not require them. One combined alarm covers both rules only where its spot meets both. The smoke alarm rules, set out on the smoke detector page, differ from the CO rules above. A combined unit also reaches its end-of-life date as one device, so both kinds of protection are replaced together.

03Does a CO alarm replace yearly furnace or boiler service?

No. An alarm warns once carbon monoxide is already in the air; it does nothing about the source. The City's CO page, citing the CDC, advises a yearly service of fuel-burning appliances by a qualified technician. It also advises a yearly chimney cleaning for a vented fireplace. Heating work is a separate trade from the alarm wiring.

04Do hardwired CO alarms need a circuit of their own?

Not usually. NFPA 72, which now covers CO alarms too, lets household alarms use a dedicated circuit or the unswitched part of a shared one. Chicago requires combination-type AFCI on every 120-volt, 15- and 20-amp dwelling-unit branch circuit, alarm circuits included (Municipal Code of Chicago 14E-2-210, read October 19, 2026). Circuits in metal conduit, EMT or MI cable with metal boxes are exempt. Highlights Chicago can check what feeds the alarms now.

05What does the number on a digital CO alarm mean?

It is the carbon monoxide level in parts per million. Alarms listed to UL 2034 are built not to sound at once for low readings. At 70 ppm the alarm must go off within 60 to 240 minutes, and at 400 ppm within 4 to 15 minutes. A rising number with no alarm still deserves attention, and anyone feeling unwell should leave and call 911.

06Will a CO alarm call the fire department or a monitoring company?

Not a standard one. A CO alarm sounds inside the home, so it protects the people who can hear it. Alerts to a phone or a central station come from connected models or from a monitoring company's contract. Highlights Chicago's part is installing and wiring the alarms; any monitoring is a separate service from a monitoring company.

07Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Chicago CO alarm rules, building by building

Three questions the blocks above leave open: rentals under the Illinois Act, boiler-heated apartments, and home sales.

What does Illinois' CO alarm law ask of the owner and the occupant in a Chicago rental?

Two sets of duties. Under the Illinois Carbon Monoxide Alarm Detector Act (430 ILCS 135), the owner installs approved alarms in working order and hands over testing and upkeep instructions. The occupant follows them. Highlights Chicago's part is the wiring when the owner chooses hardwired alarms.

Illinois has required CO alarms since January 1, 2007. The State Fire Marshal's fact sheet says local authorities enforce the Act and may add further rules, which Chicago's CO page does. So a Chicago rental with gas heat answers to both.

Chicago's CO page adds a CO-specific detail. Alarms in homes built since 2018 must be hardwired with a removable back-up battery. Occupants must keep working batteries in any alarm that needs one, so even a hardwired CO alarm leaves the occupant one battery to watch.

Highlights Chicago gives free estimates, so an owner can price hardwired CO alarms for every unit at once. On house power, a dead back-up battery no longer silences the alarm.

The State Fire Marshal's CO fact sheet, read October 17, 2026, sets out the occupant's side in full. Occupants test, replace batteries per the supplied instructions, notify the owner of problems and allow the owner access to fix them.

Does a Chicago apartment with no gas appliances need a CO alarm if the building's boiler burns gas?

Usually, under the Illinois Act. The State Fire Marshal's summary applies the Act to any structure that burns fossil fuel for heat. The City's example of an exempt home has all-electric heating, which radiators fed by a gas boiler are not. Highlights Chicago can wire hardwired alarms into each unit of a boiler-heated building.

Much of Chicago's older flat stock is heated by one gas or oil boiler in the basement. It pipes hot water or steam to radiators upstairs, while the range, dryer and water heater in a unit may all be electric.

Owners of those units sometimes reason that no flame inside the apartment means no CO alarm. The boiler room gets an alarm, and the floors above are left without one.

The Act's reach is the structure, not the apartment. The fact sheet says a structure with more than one dwelling unit needs approved alarms near every sleeping room. It sends questions about where alarms are required to the local building authority, which in Chicago is the Department of Buildings.

On permitted work, customers describe Highlights Chicago jobs passing City inspection. In a boiler-heated building, a hardwired alarm in each unit, on that unit's own circuit, gives every household its own warning.

The Cook County Assessor counts 133,662 two- to six-unit residential buildings in Chicago, the stock where one basement boiler often heats several homes.

What do CO alarms mean when a Chicago home is sold?

A sale is often when missing or expired CO alarms surface, through a home inspection or a buyer's walkthrough. Dealing with the alarms early keeps the issue out of the last weeks before closing. Highlights Chicago can wire hardwired alarms before closing.

A Chicago sale usually brings in a home inspector, who may note whether alarms exist, where they sit and how old they look.

Sellers sometimes buy a few battery alarms the week before closing and mount each wherever a screw goes in. The buyer inherits alarms in the wrong spots, some with years already spent on a store shelf.

The rules give both sides a fixed checklist. Each bedroom zone and each fuel-burning room either has an alarm or does not. The date code on the back shows how long each alarm has left.

A seller who books the work early can show the buyer's side who did it. Highlights Chicago is licensed and insured, as its Yelp business page states.
