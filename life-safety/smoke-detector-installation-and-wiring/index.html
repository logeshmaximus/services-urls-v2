Site assessment

What decides a smoke alarm job

Eight things about the home set how many alarms it needs, what powers them and how the wire gets there.

  1. 01

    Year built, and any big remodel

    The year sets which of the City's alarm rules apply. The library tab below explains the 2018 line and what a remodel changes.

  2. 02

    Bedrooms and levels

    The City wants an alarm outside each bedroom, within 15 feet of it, and at least one on every level, basement included.

  3. 03

    What powers each alarm now

    Removable batteries, a sealed battery or house wiring. A battery alarm screwed over a ceiling box with capped wires usually marks a hardwired unit that was taken down.

  4. 04

    The date on the back

    Most alarms print a manufacture date on the back. The Department of Buildings says an alarm reaches the end of its service life at 10 years, hardwired ones included.

  5. 05

    Distance from stove, bath and corners

    City rules keep ionization alarms 10 feet and photoelectric alarms 6 feet from cooking appliances. No alarm may sit within 3 feet of a door to a room with a tub or shower, or within 4 inches of a wall-ceiling corner.

  6. 06

    Combination smoke and CO units

    A combination alarm counts for both rules only where its spot meets both; the CO placement rules are on the carbon monoxide detector page linked below.

  7. 07

    Whether they sound together

    Pressing TEST on one linked alarm should set off every other one in the home. When only one sounds, the link is missing or broken.

  8. 08

    The circuit and the panel

    Hardwired alarms ride on a house circuit. Its breaker is traced, and panel space is checked if a new circuit is needed. Chicago requires combination-type AFCI on every 120-volt, 15- and 20-amp dwelling-unit branch circuit, alarm circuits included (Municipal Code of Chicago 14E-2-210, read October 19, 2026). That covers circuits modified, replaced or extended. Circuits in metal conduit, EMT or MI cable with metal boxes are exempt.

When a home has only battery alarms today

  1. 1

    Sealed 10-year units

    Where hardwiring is not required, each removable-battery alarm gives way to a sealed one. No wiring is involved.

  3. 2

    Hardwired on a new circuit

    A circuit from the panel reaches each alarm spot, with a linking wire between them, wherever the ceilings and walls leave a route.

  5. 3

    House power, wireless link

    Each alarm still gets wired for power, but the alarms signal each other by radio, so no linking wire has to cross the rooms.

Which route fits turns on the home's age, any remodel permit and how the ceilings are built.

A battery, a new alarm or new wiring?

Most smoke alarm noise is a message, not a fault.

  • A hardwired unit that chirps after an outage has usually drained its back-up battery, and a fresh one ends it.

  • An alarm past its date, or a sealed unit at the end of its life, comes down and a new one goes up.

  • Cooking false alarms point to the alarm's spot or its sensor type.

  • New wire is only needed when the alarm circuit is dead, the link has failed, or added bedrooms need alarms of their own.

A hardwired alarm nobody sells anymore

Hardwired alarms plug into a connector at the ceiling box, and makers use their own plugs. When an old model is discontinued, a replacement from another maker needs a new connector and mounting plate at each alarm, unless that maker sells an adapter for the old plug. Reading the make and model off the old alarms before buying new ones avoids a second trip.

01Does every smoke alarm in a Chicago home have to be replaced at once?

No. Each alarm is due when it fails or is past its date, and the dates on a set often differ. The catch is linking. A new hardwired alarm has to work with the ones it links to, and makers list which of their models do. That is why an old, discontinued set is often replaced together. Highlights Chicago installs the hardwired kind.

02Does Illinois law or City law set the battery rule in a Chicago home?

The State Fire Marshal's page on the Illinois Smoke Alarm Law was read October 17, 2026. It says any new smoke alarm installed in a home since January 1, 2023 must have a 10-year sealed battery. It exempts homes built after 1988 that already have hardwired alarms, and homes with wireless integrated alarms. The Department of Buildings publishes the City's own dates, set out in the comparison table above.

03Is it safe to swap a hardwired smoke alarm without an electrician?

A hardwired alarm sits on a 120-volt house circuit, and the wires in its ceiling box stay live until the right breaker is off. Sealed-battery units screw on with no wiring at all. A hardwired swap, a new box or a new circuit is work for a licensed electrical contractor such as Highlights Chicago. A required alarm should never be left disconnected: the City treats tampering as a crime.

04Who replaces the smoke alarms in a Chicago rental unit?

The owner provides the required alarms and replaces any that fail or pass their replacement date, the Department of Buildings says. The occupant's part is keeping any batteries working, testing the alarms and reporting a dead one to the owner in writing. Owners must also test the alarms in shared halls, stairways and laundry rooms on a regular basis.

05Can smart smoke alarms be used in a Chicago home?

Yes, within the City's power rules. A hardwired smart alarm takes the house circuit and a back-up battery like any other. Some add a phone alert for when no one is home. A smart model that runs only on removable batteries falls under the removable-battery rule in the comparison table above.

06What smoke alarms suit a deaf or hard-of-hearing resident?

Alarms that wake a sleeper without sound: a strobe light, or a bed shaker under the pillow or mattress. Some hardwired strobe models link with the same maker's standard alarms, so a bedroom unit flashes when any alarm in the home sounds. A hardwired strobe mounts on a ceiling or wall box like any hardwired alarm, so Highlights Chicago can wire one into the existing alarm circuit.

07Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

How Chicago's smoke alarm rules change with a home's age

Chicago does not ask every home for the same alarms. These questions follow the rules in the Department of Buildings' guidance and the City's permit terms, and what they mean for the wiring.

Does a Chicago home built before 2018 need a smoke alarm inside each bedroom?

Not unless it was built or significantly remodeled in 2018 or later. Older homes need alarms near the bedrooms and on each level, but not inside the sleeping rooms. Homes on the newer side add one in every bedroom, all linked. When the remodel is on a City permit, Highlights Chicago is the contractor of record on the City permit.

In a typical Chicago bungalow or flat, the bedrooms open off one short hall. One hall alarm served them all, plus one per level, and the City still accepts that layout in an older home.

When a remodel reworks the bedrooms, the plans tend to keep the hall alarm where it was and add nothing inside the rooms.

A significant remodel puts the home under the newer layout. The City's terms for nonstructural interior work, read October 17, 2026, add a second trigger. A permit for work in a residential unit requires smoke alarms and carbon monoxide detectors throughout it, hardwired in most cases.

Each new bedroom alarm needs power and a link to the others. Both run through the joists from the hall, or from an attic or basement, while the ceilings are open. West Town, the community area where Highlights Chicago holds the most City permits, sits in the township with the city's highest prewar share.

The Cook County Assessor dates 83.0% of the houses and small residential buildings in West Chicago township, which includes West Town, to before 1940.

Can a hardwired smoke alarm in Chicago be swapped for a 10-year sealed battery unit?

Usually not. A home under the City's hardwiring rule must keep hardwired alarms with a battery back-up. The battery rules in the comparison table above never covered them. Sealed 10-year alarms replace removable-battery units. A hardwired swap is licensed electrical work, and Highlights Chicago is a City-licensed electrical contractor.

Chicago's battery rules changed in this decade. In 2020 the City Council voted to phase out alarms powered only by a removable battery.

Hardware store shelves put sealed and hardwired alarms side by side. A sealed unit screwed over a hardwired ceiling box looks like an easy upgrade, with no electrician, and the old wires get capped.

That swap breaks two things. The home is still under the hardwiring rule. And a sealed unit on a capped box drops out of the link, so a fire elsewhere no longer sets it off.

The City's license page, read October 17, 2026, says a license is needed to install, alter or maintain wiring under the Chicago Electrical Code. That holds whether or not a permit is required. A hardwired swap is that kind of work.

The City's licensed contractor register showed Highlights Chicago's licenses, ECC96456 and ECC94521, active when checked on October 2, 2026.

Which smoke alarm sensor suits a Chicago condo where the kitchen sits near the bedrooms?

Usually a photoelectric alarm. In an open-plan condo or a small flat, the alarm the City wants near the bedrooms can end up a few steps from the stove. Photoelectric sensors react less to cooking smoke, and City rules let them sit closer to cooking appliances than ionization alarms. Moving a hardwired alarm away from the stove is electrical work for a licensed contractor like Highlights Chicago.

A condo kitchen can open straight onto the living space, and a flat carved from an older building can have short halls.

The usual alarm is whatever the store had, mounted where the old one was. When it sounds at every dinner, households pull the battery or cover the alarm, and the home loses its warning.

The Department of Buildings treats that as a sensor and placement problem. It notes that newer alarms must have a silence button, and that both sensor types detect common household fires in time to escape.

For a hardwired alarm, a new spot means a new ceiling box and a wire run from the old one. The distances in the checklist above set where it may go. Highlights Chicago gives free estimates.

The City's smoke alarm page, read October 17, 2026, lists four fixes for an alarm that sounds at cooking. Keep it at least 10 feet from the stove. Use a model with a silence button. Move a ceiling alarm to the wall. Or replace an ionization alarm with a photoelectric one.
