Surge symptoms and causes

Type 1, Type 2 or Type 3: what changes?

The UL 1449 safety standard sorts surge devices by where they connect, and NEC Article 285 covers how they are installed. A common setup in a house is a Type 2 at the panel plus a few Type 3 strips.

Type 1 (service side)

Where it connectsAhead of the main breaker, at the meter or service disconnect
What it coversThe whole service, before the panel
Who installs itA licensed electrician
Usual homeA service with an outside disconnect

Type 2 (at the panel)

Where it connectsOn a breaker in the panel, or as a surge breaker
What it coversEvery circuit the panel feeds
Who installs itA licensed electrician
Usual homeAlmost any house or unit with a breaker panel

Type 3 (point of use)

Where it connectsAt the outlet, as a strip or a surge receptacle
What it coversOnly what is plugged into it
Who installs itThe homeowner, for a plug-in strip
Usual homeComputers, TVs and network gear

Layers, not one box

A panel device does not retire the power strips.

The panel device takes the large surges that arrive on the wiring. What gets past it, and what arrives on cable or phone lines, is handled closer to the equipment.

  • At the service or panel

    Clamps large surges before they spread to every circuit in the house.

  • Where the cable and phone lines enter

    A small protector on each line where it comes into the building.

  • At the equipment

    Strips or surge receptacles for the computer, the TV and the router.

How many layers a home needs depends on what is plugged in and how its lines come into the building.

Site assessment

What gets checked before a surge device goes in

The device is the small part of the job. These checks decide whether it will have a working path for a surge, and where it should sit.

Illustrative photo

  1. 01

    Panel make and free spaces

    A wired device lands on a two-pole breaker. A full panel narrows the choices.

  2. 02

    How short the leads can run

    Every extra inch of wire between the device and its breaker weakens the protection. The mounting spot is picked to keep the leads short and straight.

  3. 03

    The grounding electrode

    The check looks for the clamp on the water pipe and for a ground rod outside, and whether both still reach the panel.

  4. 04

    The bond across the water meter

    A jumper wire across the water meter keeps the pipe ground continuous whenever the meter is pulled.

  5. 05

    Overhead or underground service

    An overhead drop from the alley sits in the open, where lightning and falling limbs reach it. The mast and weatherhead get a look as well.

  6. 06

    Cable, phone and fiber entry

    A line that enters far from the panel needs its protector tied to the same ground. Two separate grounds let a surge cross through the equipment between them.

  7. 07

    What the circuits feed

    Furnace and boiler boards, central air, an induction range and network gear set how much protection makes sense.

  8. 08

    Solar, battery or generator connections

    A transfer switch or an inverter is another way in for a surge, and can call for a device of its own.

If the panel can't take a wired device

  1. 1

    A surge breaker

    Where the panel maker offers one, it does the same job without a separate box beside the panel.

  3. 2

    A device at the service disconnect

    A Type 1 unit protects at the service instead, when the disconnect sits outside the panel.

  5. 3

    A new panel first

    A fuse box, or a panel with no matching device on the market, is replaced, and the protection goes into the new one.

Which route fits depends on the panel make, the free spaces and how the service is laid out.

Replace the device, or fix what is behind it?

A spent surge device is replaced, not repaired.

  • If its light went out in a storm, it did its job, and the usual replacement is the same type in the same spot.

  • If devices or boards keep failing, the ground, the bond and the connections behind the device get checked before another one goes in.

Failures with no storm at all

Lights that dim and brighten, or electronics failing on calm days, point to a loose neutral or a poor ground rather than surges. A surge device fixes neither. A loose service neutral can push one side of the house well above normal voltage and needs an electrician promptly. Highlights Chicago takes emergency electrical calls 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

01Does every Chicago home need a whole-house surge protector?

No. The value depends on what is wired in. A furnace or boiler control board, central air, an induction range or a home office can be a lot to lose in one storm. A house with gas heat, window units and few electronics has far less. Highlights Chicago gives free estimates, so the panel and the ground can be looked at before anyone decides.

02Will a whole-house surge protector survive a direct lightning strike?

No surge device stops a direct strike on the building; that is the job of a lightning protection system on the roof. A surge device handles the far more common case: a strike nearby, utility switching or a storm on the overhead wires. Each sends a short spike down the service. Even then, it limits the spike rather than removing it.

03How long does a whole-house surge device last?

There is no fixed number of years. Each surge it absorbs wears the parts inside a little. One stormy summer can use up a device that would otherwise run for a long time. That is why the indicator matters more than the install date. A glance at it after every major storm is a two-second habit worth keeping.

04Does a panel device protect a charger in a detached garage?

Partly. A garage fed by a long run from the house can pick up surges along the way. A second device at the garage subpanel is worth pricing. EV charger work is part of Highlights Chicago's own record: 28 of its 486 stand-alone electrical permits, across all years, are EV charger jobs.

05Does homeowners insurance cover electronics lost to a surge?

It depends on the policy and on the cause, so the insurer is the one to ask. Photos of the failed equipment and its model labels help any claim, along with the date of the storm and when the power came back. The failed items are worth keeping until the insurer says otherwise.

06Can a homeowner wire in a whole-house surge protector?

Not in Chicago. The City's electrical contractor license page, read October 17, 2026, says a City license is required for any wiring or equipment the Chicago Electrical Code regulates. That applies whether or not a permit is needed. A panel-mounted surge device is that kind of work. Highlights Chicago holds City licenses ECC96456 and ECC94521.

07Does a surge protector lower the electric bill?

No. A surge device sits idle until a spike arrives and draws almost no power, so it neither saves nor costs anything on the ComEd bill. Plug-in gadgets sold as power savers make claims the electric meter does not bear out. Real savings come from the equipment that uses the power, such as a heat pump, LED lighting or better insulation.

08Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Guide 04 / 04

How surge protection fits a Chicago building

A surge device is one link in a chain that runs from the street to the ground rod. In Chicago the weak links are usually old, and the code is not the one national articles describe.

Why does the ground decide whether a surge device works in an old Chicago building?

The Cook County Assessor dates 58.6% of Chicago's houses and small residential buildings to before 1940. Buildings that old were often grounded to the metal water pipe where it came in from the street.

The usual surge job bolts a device into the panel and stops there. But the device works by sending the surge to ground, so it is only as good as that path.

The path has often changed without anyone noticing. A replaced water service, a plastic repair or a new water meter can leave the old clamp attached to nothing that reaches the earth.

Highlights Chicago's stand-alone electrical permits lean toward exactly this work. Of its 486, 281 are panel or service jobs, the work in which the ground and the bond are made.

A July 2022 review from Andrew L. shows how it can look. Two Highlights electricians traced flickering lights in the house to grounding that had been missing for years, and the work was finished within two days.

Does the national surge rule apply in Chicago?

National articles often say surge protection is now required on homes. That comes from NEC section 230.67, added in the 2020 edition, which calls for a surge device on new and replaced dwelling services.

So a buyer can fairly assume a Chicago panel upgrade includes one by law, or that an older home without one is out of code.

Chicago writes its own electrical code. The City's code page, read October 17, 2026, says the Chicago Electrical Code (Title 14E) incorporates provisions of the 2017 National Electrical Code. That edition came before section 230.67.

Unless a Chicago amendment says otherwise, the 2020 rule should not be taken as a Chicago requirement, and the City's code text is the authority. Highlights Chicago works under that code day to day: it filed 392 of its 1,223 City permits since 2012 itself, as the applicant.

That record can be checked. The City of Chicago publishes its building permits as open data, searchable by contractor name, so the permits behind these figures are public.

Who protects the shared equipment in a condo or two-flat?

In a two-flat or condo building, each unit usually has its own meter and panel. A house meter feeds the hallways, the boiler, the intercom and any shared pumps.

A unit owner who adds a surge device protects that unit's circuits and nothing else. The boiler board, the door buzzer and the common lights sit on the house panel, out of that device's reach.

The equipment most exposed to a storm is often the shared kind, and replacing it is a building expense. A device on the house panel, or a Type 1 unit at the building service, is a decision for the owners together.
