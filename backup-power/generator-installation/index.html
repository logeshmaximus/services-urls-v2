Whole house or essential circuits?

Backing up everything is a choice, not a requirement.

The generator's size follows the circuits it has to carry. Fewer circuits mean a smaller unit, a smaller gas demand and often a simpler switch.

  • Whole house

    Every circuit on one automatic switch, with the generator sized for central air and the range running together.

  • Managed whole house

    Every circuit connected, with load management that sheds big appliances in turn so a smaller unit keeps up.

  • Essential circuits

    Heat, the refrigerator, the sump pump and a few lights move to a subpanel that only the generator feeds.

The load calculation decides which of the three a given panel and service can support.

Site assessment

What gets checked before a generator is sized

A generator is sized from the house, not from a brochure. These readings and measurements settle the size, the spot and most of the price.

Illustrative photo

  1. 01

    Connected load and service size

    A load calculation under NEC Article 220 adds up what runs at once. It sets the generator's size, air-cooled for most houses or liquid-cooled for the largest loads, and shows whether the service can carry it.

  2. 02

    Panel make, age and free space

    An interlock kit has to be listed for the panel it fits, and a transfer switch needs breaker space or a feeder tap. A full or discontinued panel changes the route.

  3. 03

    Where the unit can sit

    Manufacturers set clearances from windows, doors, vents and walls. On a narrow Chicago lot those distances often pick the spot.

  4. 04

    Gas meter and line size

    In Chicago the gas meter belongs to Peoples Gas, the city's gas utility. A generator at full load can need a larger meter, which is requested from Peoples Gas and comes with its own wait.

  5. 05

    The route from unit to switch

    Brick walls, gangways and finished basements decide how the feeder gets from the pad to the transfer switch. The Chicago Electrical Code generally requires the wiring to run in raceway, such as conduit, and allows the plastic-sheathed cable common in the suburbs only in narrow cases.

  6. 06

    Grounding and the neutral

    The generator's neutral and ground have to match the transfer device. Some setups switch the neutral and some do not, and the wiring follows.

  7. 07

    Loads that start hard

    Central air, pumps and some ranges draw a surge on startup. A unit sized only for running watts can stall when they kick in.

  8. 08

    Building and association rules

    Condo boards, landlords and shared lots can limit where a unit goes and when the work happens.

If the panel can't take a transfer switch

  1. 1

    An interlock on the existing panel

    An interlock and an inlet box serve a portable generator without moving circuits.

  3. 2

    A switch with its own subpanel

    The essential circuits move to a new subpanel fed through the transfer switch, and the old panel stays in place.

  5. 3

    A service-rated switch at the meter

    A transfer switch rated as the service disconnect sits between the meter and the panel, so the whole house transfers at once.

  7. 4

    A panel or service upgrade

    When the panel is full, obsolete or too small anyway, the upgrade and the generator hookup are planned as one job.

Which route fits depends on the panel make, the free spaces, the service rating and the load calculation.

Repair, replace or add on?

A dead battery, a tripped breaker or a loose control wire is a repair.

  • An old unit that keeps failing, or one too small for the house as it is now, is the point to price a replacement.

  • Central air or an EV charger added since the install can call for load management rather than a bigger generator.

A generator that came with the house

Plenty of Chicago houses change hands with a standby unit already in the yard. The permit number, the generator's make and model and the transfer switch label show what was installed. A unit with no City permit on record is worth an inspection before anyone relies on it.

Incentives

Neither of the IRS's home energy credit pages lists a standby generator. The Residential Clean Energy Credit, which covered home battery storage, is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, and the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit covers improvements made through December 31, 2025 (IRS, read October 17, 2026). ComEd's rebate pages, read October 19, 2026, list no offer for standby generators or transfer switches, and ComEd notes that offers change (comed.com).

01Does every home need a whole-house generator?

No. The outages that damage a Chicago house come from a few circuits. A sump pump can stop in a storm, or a furnace or boiler can go cold in January and let pipes freeze. Those circuits are the floor, and everything above them is comfort. Highlights Chicago gives free estimates, so both levels of coverage can be priced before the household decides.

02Does a standby generator need a City permit in Chicago?

Yes. A permanently installed generator and its transfer equipment are electrical work that the City permits and inspects. A generator wired without a permit can surface at a home sale or during an insurance claim. It can also fail at the first outage, when the transfer equipment has to work and nobody has checked it.

03What size generator does a Chicago house need?

Size comes from the connected load, not the square footage. Central air, an electric range and pumps decide most of it. Air-cooled home standby units are commonly sold from roughly 10 to 26 kW, and liquid-cooled units serve larger loads. An older bungalow with gas heat can need far less than a newer house with electric heat.

04Can a portable generator plug into a dryer outlet?

No. A cord with plugs on both ends can push generator power back through the meter onto the street wires and injure line workers. It also leaves the exposed prongs live. The safe hookup is an inlet box outside and a transfer switch or listed interlock at the panel, so only one source can ever feed the house.

05How does the gas work fit with the electrical work on a generator?

Coordination comes down to timing and access. The gas contractor needs the pad location and the unit's fuel requirement before running pipe. The generator cannot be started until the gas is connected. The electrical side runs from the wiring at the pad to the transfer switch. Before booking, agree with Highlights Chicago on who hires the gas contractor.

06Natural gas or propane for a Chicago generator?

Most Chicago standby generators run on natural gas from the street main. A propane tank needs yard space and refills that a city lot often lacks. Propane suits a property without a large enough gas service. The fuel is chosen before the unit, since many models are rated for slightly less output on natural gas than on propane.

07Does ComEd have to approve a standby generator?

Usually not. A transfer switch or interlock keeps the generator separated from ComEd's lines, so the two never run in parallel. ComEd is involved when the service itself changes, such as a larger service or a moved meter. A service-rated switch at the meter is service-entrance work, so ComEd disconnects and reconnects the service for it.

08What should happen if the power is out right now?

A portable generator belongs outside, well away from windows and doors, and never in a garage or basement. Nothing should be plugged into a wall outlet to feed the house. A burning smell or smoke at the panel calls for leaving the house and calling 911 from outside. A downed line calls for staying well away from it and calling ComEd.

09Is a home battery a better choice than a generator?

It depends on the outage being planned for. A battery is silent, needs no gas line and switches over almost instantly, but it runs down, so long outages favor a generator. Some homes pair the two. Both connect at the main panel, and Highlights Chicago also offers solar battery installation, so the two can be priced side by side.

10Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

How backup power fits a Chicago house

A standby generator works for a few hours a year, so the details that decide whether it works are easy to skip. These three come up most in Chicago buildings.

Why does the sump pump change the plan in a Chicago basement?

Chicago's housing is old: 58.6% of its houses and small residential buildings were built before 1940, according to the Cook County Assessor. Many have basements with a sump or ejector pump, and the storms that cut power can also raise the water.

The usual approach picks a generator size first and sorts out the circuits later. In an older basement that is where it goes wrong. A sump pump often shares a circuit with the washer or a freezer and cannot simply be moved to backup.

The circuit layout therefore comes before the generator size. A pump on its own circuit moves easily to an essential-circuit subpanel. Some households also add a battery backup pump for the moments before the generator transfers.

Highlights Chicago's five busiest community areas for City permits are West Town, Logan Square, Near West Side, Lake View and Lincoln Square. In the 24 months to September 2026, 59.6% of the City's panel upgrade permits matched to an Assessor residential record were on buildings from before 1940.

What should a two-flat or condo owner settle first?

In a two-flat or condo building, each unit usually has its own meter and panel. The building often has a house meter too, for hallways, the boiler or shared pumps.

The usual mistake is to wire a generator to whichever panel is handiest. A generator connects behind one meter, so that choice decides whose circuits it backs up. The shared loads a building most wants on backup may sit on the house meter.

Backing up the boiler controls or a common sump pump is therefore a building decision rather than one owner's. Highlights Chicago works on meter banks and panel replacements in multi-unit buildings, as the job photos on this page show.

The stakes are citywide: the Cook County Assessor dates 88.5% of Chicago's two to six unit buildings to before 1940.

Why do some Chicago blocks lose power more often than others?

Many Chicago blocks are fed by overhead lines along the alley, with a service drop to each building. Ice, wind and falling branches hit overhead wires harder than buried ones, so some blocks go dark in storms that others ride out.

The usual assumption is that ComEd restores power and the outage is over. But ComEd owns the drop wires, while the mast, the weatherhead and the meter socket on the building belong to the owner. A mast pulled off the wall by ice has to be repaired on the house side before ComEd can reconnect.
