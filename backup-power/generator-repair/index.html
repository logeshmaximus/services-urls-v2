Where a Standby System Fails on the Electrical Side

A home standby system is a chain: the generator outside, a transfer switch that moves the house over, a breaker and feeder between them, a charger that keeps the starting battery ready, and the panel that carries the load. From indoors, a break anywhere in that chain looks the same.

  • Standby generator

    The engine, alternator and controller

  • Main panel

    Feeds the circuits that run on backup

  • Meter and service entrance

    Where ComEd power enters the house

  • Feeder and conduit

    The run between the unit and the switch

  • Safety disconnect

    A lockable shutoff on an equipment feed

  • Transfer switch

    Moves the house between ComEd and the unit

Repair the transfer switch, or replace it?

A worn transfer switch does not always need replacing.

Contacts, control boards and lugs can be repaired on many switches. Heat damage, a rating that does not suit the service, or a model whose parts are no longer made is where a new switch makes sense.

  • Repair in place

    A loose lug, a failed control board or a worn contactor on a switch whose parts are still made.

  • Replace the switch

    Heat damage, an undersized rating, or no parts left. A new switch is permitted work with a City inspection.

  • Replace the generator

    When the engine side is past repair, the switch and feeder can stay if they suit the new unit.

What a transfer test and electrical check cover

A standby system that waits for months hides its electrical faults until it has to carry the house. These checks bring them out on a calm day.

Illustrative photo

  1. 01

    Transfer switch contacts and lugs

    Heat marks, loose lugs and a contactor that chatters show up on inspection. A thermal camera picks out a hot connection while the switch carries load.

  2. 02

    A transfer with the house on it

    A controlled outage test, with utility power cut at the switch or the main, moves the house onto the generator and back again.

  3. 03

    Generator breaker against today's loads

    Central air, an EV charger or an induction range added later can overload a unit sized years earlier. The breaker and the circuits on backup are compared with the house as it is now.

  4. 04

    The feeder where it enters

    Where the feeder leaves the pad and enters the wall, conduit can crack, take on water or be chewed through. A damaged run is traced and tested before the switch takes the blame.

  5. 05

    The panel behind the switch

    The breakers that feed the switch, and the free spaces left in the panel, decide whether a repair can stay at the switch.

  6. 06

    Enclosure openings and control wiring

    Snow, leaves and nests block the air openings, and rodents chew control wiring where it enters the unit.

When the test turns up a fault

  1. 1

    Repaired on the electrical side

    A breaker, contactor, control board, lug or charger circuit is put right, and the rest stays in service.

  3. 2

    Sent to the engine side

    Faults inside the engine, alternator or controller are engine-side work, as the table above sets out.

The test result decides which side of that line a fault sits on.

01Can a generator be fixed during an outage?

Often, when the fault is electrical. A failed generator breaker, a loose switch connection or a dead charger supply can often be put right while the grid is down. That electrical side is Highlights Chicago's part of the system. An engine fault takes longer, and the portable generator answer below covers what keeps the house going meanwhile.

02What if the generator smells of gas or something is burning?

A gas odor near the unit means moving well away from it and calling Peoples Gas or 911 from a safe distance. Nothing nearby should be switched on or off. A burning smell or smoke at the transfer switch or the panel calls for leaving the house and calling 911 from outside. Nobody should open the enclosure while the engine runs or keep resetting a fault that returns.

03Can a standby generator run for days without a break?

On natural gas it can keep running as long as the supply holds, but the engine still needs attention. Many manufacturers call for an oil check after each day of continuous running. After a multi-day Chicago outage, the hours meter is worth noting. The next engine service may now fall due well ahead of the calendar.

04Why did the furnace stay off when the house was on generator power?

Either the furnace circuit is not wired through the transfer switch, or the furnace rejected the power. Many gas furnaces and boilers use electronic controls that check for correct polarity and a solid ground. A neutral and ground set up wrong for the switch can make them lock out. That fix sits in the wiring, which is Highlights Chicago's side of the work.

05What keeps the house going while the generator waits for a part?

A portable generator can carry the essentials, connected only through an outdoor inlet and a listed interlock or transfer switch, never through a wall outlet. It runs outdoors, well away from windows, doors and vents, never in a garage, basement or gangway against the house. Battery carbon monoxide alarms should be working inside. A battery backup on the sump pump protects a basement on its own.

06How often should a standby generator be serviced?

Most makers set a weekly or every-other-week exercise run. A full engine service follows at least once a year, or sooner after a set number of run hours. The electrical side does not wear on a calendar. A transfer test after a long outage shows whether the switch and breaker still carry the house.

07Who should be called first, the electrician or the generator dealer?

It depends on the symptom. A fault code, low oil or an engine that cranks without firing points to the generator maker's dealer or an independent service company. A smell of gas points to Peoples Gas. A dark house with the engine running, a tripping generator breaker or a battery that keeps dying points to an electrician such as Highlights Chicago.

08Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Guide 04 / 04

Where generator faults meet a Chicago building

A standby generator is judged on the few hours a year it actually carries the house. These three questions decide whether those hours go well.

When does a generator problem turn out to be panel or service work?

When the fault follows the power back past the transfer switch. A generator breaker that trips under modest load, heat at the panel lugs, or no listed breaker for the generator all point there. Most of Highlights Chicago's stand-alone electrical permits are panel and service work, so tracing a fault from the switch into the panel is familiar ground.

A standby generator reaches the house through the main panel or through a switch at the meter. Either way it shares the building's service, and every weakness in it.

The usual repair treats the generator as the whole problem: a new breaker, a new board, another battery. When the real fault sits in the panel, the same breaker trips again or the same connection overheats.

The switch can only deliver what the panel behind it will take. A worn bus, a crowded panel or a breaker that no longer matches its panel turns a generator call into panel work.

Highlights Chicago's stand-alone electrical work is concentrated in that part of a building: the panel and service that a transfer switch connects to.

What does a Chicago winter do to a standby generator?
