Already have solar? Three ways to build on it

An older array can usually stay on the roof.

Panels tend to outlast the electronics and wiring around them, so most changes reuse what is already up there.

  • Repair the failed part

    A dead optimizer, a burnt connector or a worn breaker is replaced on its own, and the rest of the array keeps working.

  • Add panels

    More panels need headroom in the inverter, space on the roof, a new City permit and an updated ComEd application.

  • Add a battery

    Storage keeps chosen circuits running in an outage, and it adds its own equipment and wiring at the panel.

Which route fits depends on the age of the inverter, the space in the panel and what the household wants the system to do next.

Site assessment

What a solar site assessment looks at

Most surprises on a solar job sit below the roof line. These checks shape the quote before any panel is ordered.

  1. 01

    Roof age and condition

    A roof close to the end of its life is better replaced before any panels go up.

  2. 02

    Shade and roof direction

    Trees, taller neighbors, chimneys and parapets all cut output. South, east and west faces work; north faces produce least.

  3. 03

    Roof framing

    Ballasted racks add weight to a flat roof. Older framing may need a structural review before the layout is final.

  4. 04

    Busbar and main breaker

    NEC 705.12, as adopted in the Chicago Electrical Code, caps the main breaker plus the solar breaker at 120% of the busbar rating. The library tab works through a 100-amp example.

  5. 05

    Service size and load

    A load calculation shows whether the service can carry the house, any planned EV charger and the solar connection together.

  6. 06

    Which account the array offsets

    In a building with a meter bank, the owners decide which meter the system sits behind before anything is wired.

  7. 07

    The wiring route down

    Masonry walls, parapets and finished rooms decide how conduit travels from the roof to the inverter, and how much of it shows.

  8. 08

    Grounding

    Racking, panels and the inverter bond back to the building's grounding system, which some older homes have only in part.

When the panel cannot take the solar connection

  1. 1

    A smaller array

    Sizing the system to what the busbar allows avoids panel work, at the cost of some output.

  3. 2

    A lower-rated main breaker

    Where the load calculation leaves room, a smaller main breaker frees busbar capacity for the solar breaker.

  5. 3

    A connection ahead of the main

    Tying in between the meter and the main breaker sidesteps the busbar limit, where the equipment and ComEd's rules permit it.

  7. 4

    A new panel or service

    Replacing an old or full panel settles the solar question and adds room for other loads at the same time.

The route comes from the busbar rating, the open breaker spaces and the load calculation.

Fix the part, or replace the system?

Most faults are one part, not the whole array.

  • A connector, an optimizer or a breaker is a repair.

  • An inverter still under its warranty goes back through the manufacturer.

  • Cracked panels, a discontinued inverter and tired wiring all at once is when a replacement deserves a price next to the repair.

When the original installer has closed

For systems that went through Illinois Shines, the state's solar program keeps a shortlist of installers who help stranded customers with repairs (illinoisshines.com, read October 17, 2026). Panel and inverter warranties usually sit with the manufacturer rather than the installer.

01Do solar panels on a Chicago roof need regular servicing?

Very little. Rain and melting snow keep most Chicago arrays clean enough, and the panels have no moving parts. There is no need for a washing contract or a yearly service visit. A look at the monitoring app now and then, and after a big storm, catches most problems. Highlights Chicago, a City-licensed electrical contractor, can then test the solar breaker, disconnects and wiring back to the panel.

02What happens to the panels when the roof is replaced?

They come off for the roofers. The system is shut down at its disconnects and the panels and racking are stored. The roof is then redone around new or resealed mounts. Afterward the array goes back up, and its wiring and connectors are tested before it is switched on again. Booking the roofer and the electrician together keeps the house from sitting without solar for weeks.

03Does snow stop solar panels from working in a Chicago winter?

Winter output is low mainly because Chicago days are short and the sun sits low, so December produces a fraction of a summer month. Cold does not harm the panels, and bright cold days can be productive. The real hazard is people. Climbing onto an icy roof to clear panels risks a fall and can scratch the glass, so most owners leave them alone.

04What should happen after hail or a storm damages the panels?

Stay off the roof. A cracked panel still makes voltage whenever light hits it, so damaged cables can be live in daylight. A marked rapid shutdown switch is safe to turn off. Photos taken from the ground help an insurance claim. Smoke or a burning smell means leaving the building and calling 911. Before a restart, a licensed electrician such as Highlights Chicago should check the wiring and disconnects.

05Can solar panels charge an electric car?

Indirectly. The array feeds the house panel and the car charges from that panel. When the car charges at midday on a sunny day, much of that energy can come from the roof. When it charges overnight, the power comes from ComEd, and the daytime solar was credited earlier. The scheduling setting in most chargers or car apps can shift charging into sunny hours.

06How big a solar system does a Chicago home need?

It depends on use more than on the roof. Twelve months of ComEd bills give the household's yearly kilowatt-hours, and the array is sized against that figure, the usable roof area and the shade. A planned electric car, induction range or heat pump raises the target. A home energy audit from Highlights Chicago beforehand can trim the use the array has to cover.

07What changes on a commercial solar installation?

Mostly scale and the service. Many commercial buildings have three-phase power, which needs a three-phase inverter or several units balanced across the phases. Bigger arrays can mean a structural review of the roof and a longer ComEd review. Landlords, renters and roof warranties all add people to the approval. The electrical connection still follows the same code rules as a house.

08Do you cover my part of Chicago?

We work citywide, with the largest share of our permit record in Wards 1, 32, 26, 47 and 27 on the north and northwest sides. We also hold current registration in Evanston and Palatine.

Why the age matters electrically

A building from 1906 was wired, if it was wired at all, for lighting. Everything since — the refrigerator, the window units, the home office, the induction range, the car — has been added to a service that was never designed for it. That is the single thread running through most Chicago electrical work.

Township by township

West Chicago is the oldest at 83% pre-1940 across 65,664 properties. Lake View follows at 71% across 23,046. Jefferson township — Portage Park, Jefferson Park, Irving Park — is younger at 60% pre-1940 but far larger at 112,923 properties, and its median year of 1927 puts most of it in the bungalow era. Rogers Park is the youngest of the north-side townships at a 1938 median.

What that means room by room

188,389 properties citywide are two-storey and 154,926 single-storey; 324,305 have full basements, which is where the panel usually lives and where access is usually straightforward. 107,336 properties have no garage at all — relevant the moment an EV charger enters the conversation. And 335,769 use a warm-air furnace against 98,849 on hot water or steam, which decides what kind of equipment circuits exist already.

Solar on Chicago buildings: what the panel, the records and the code add

Two things trip up Chicago solar projects more often than the panels do. One is the main panel the array feeds; the other is the paper trail the system leaves.

Why do so many Chicago solar projects stall at the main panel?

Chicago's housing is old. Cook County Assessor data puts 58.6% of the city's houses and small residential buildings before 1940, decades before anyone planned for power flowing back from a roof. Small services are still going in, too: in the 24 months to September 2026, City permits listed 1,401 new 100-amp services.

On a 100-amp panel with a 100-amp main breaker, the busbar rule leaves room for a 20-amp solar breaker. That carries about 3.8 kW of inverter output at 240 volts, often less than the roof could hold. The routes listed under the assessment show how more room is opened.

A panel or service change in Chicago needs a City-licensed electrical contractor, and so does the solar connection. Highlights Chicago holds two City electrical contractor licenses, ECC96456 and ECC94521, and its breaker box and panel repair service covers the panel side.

How can a buyer check the solar on a Chicago house before closing?

A rooftop array is permitted electrical work in Chicago, and the City publishes its building permits as open data. A system on a house should leave a trail there.

A search of the City's building permit data by address shows whether the system was permitted, when, and which electrical contractor was on the permit. A system with no permit is a question for the seller before closing, not after. Highlights Chicago's own permits can be looked up the same way.

Ownership and billing matter as much as the permit. An owned system transfers with the house; a leased one comes with a contract the buyer takes over. ComEd says a system that had full retail net metering before 2025 keeps that status for its lifetime, and later owners inherit it. The inverter label and the monitoring account show what is up there and how it has performed.

What does rapid shutdown mean for an older Chicago array?

The Chicago Electrical Code, Title 14E of the Municipal Code, incorporates the 2017 National Electrical Code (chicago.gov, read October 17, 2026). Its Article 690.12 requires rooftop solar to shut down quickly, so firefighters do not meet live wiring on a roof. Tighter limits apply inside the array itself.

Arrays approved under the City's earlier electrical code may have no rapid shutdown at all, or only a switch at the inverter. They met the rules in force when they were approved.

The rule comes back when the system changes. Replacing the inverter, adding panels or reinstalling the array after a new roof can bring the changed parts under the current code. That is why an upgrade quote for an older system sometimes includes shutdown devices under the panels. A solar inverter installation is treated as permitted work for the same reason.
